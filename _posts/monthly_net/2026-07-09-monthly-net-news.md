## This month in Amateur Radio History

- July 1914
 The Amateur Radio Relay League was founded by Hiram Percy Maxim.

- July 2nd, 1921
 Amateur Radio Broadcasters started setting up their own little broadcast stations, and by the end of 1921, it was estimated that 1200 amateurs had made at least one broadcast. Some had regular programs and schedules and would become the first commercial stations. However in July 1921, over 400,000 people listened into commentators on the Dempsey-Carpentier boxing match. Radio sales approached 100,000 per near, not counting crystal sets which were selling at 20,000 a month! This might become a problem for the fledging amateurs…

- July 24, 1924
  The Department of Commerce authorized new amateur frequency bands. They were 150 to 200 meters (1.500 to 2.000 Mhz), 75 to 80 meters (3.500 to 4.000 Mhz), 40 to 43 meters (7.000 to 7.500 Mhz), 20 to 22 meters (13.600 to 15.000 Mhz), and 4 to 5 meters (60.000 to 75.000 Mhz). Obviously these have shifted a hair… Except for a portion of the 150 to 200 meter band, spark was prohibited. A spark station on 200 meters actually generated a signal from 150 to 250 meters. Since CW took only a fraction of the bandwidth that spark did, over 50 CW stations in the same area could occupy the 150 to 250 meter range vs one spark station. Spark would survive in the hands of a few rebels until 1927 when it was banned altogether. CW was here to stay.

- July 1954
  The first amateur two way contact with the Moon was achieved on 1296 Mhz via [Moon Bounce by the Rhododendron Swamp VHF Society](https://www.rfcafe.com/references/electronics-world/ham-radio-earth-moon-earth-contact-october-1960-electronics-world.htm) in Massachusetts. It was noticed in 1954 that 2 m signals were reflected by the moon. In 1954, the Naval Research Laboratory was able to accomplish a moon bounce.

- July 1970
  During protests against the Vietnam war on many college campuses, a ham radio net appeared on 7.260 and 14.294 MHz used to gather and exchange information on what was happening on campuses nationwide. The net was so good at this they began to feed news to wire services and major networks. However, as talks became heated on the merits of the war, that it started coordinating funds, strike activities (including shipping draft cards to DC for a massive bonfire) and boycott activities. Then deliberate jamming and name calling started. The controversy led to an [article in the July 1970 issue of QST](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/Archive-DX/QST/60s/QST-1970-07.pdf) (page 9) on the use of Amateur bands for heated political discussion, and the capability of broadcasts going international could have negative political consequences. ARRL condemned both the Jammers calling them “frontier justice vigilantes”, and the use of the air waves for political discussions including arguing about the Vietnam War. Letters poured into QST with a 2:1 ratio against using Ham Radio for political discussion.
