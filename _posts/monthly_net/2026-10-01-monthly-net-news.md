## This Month In Amateur Radio History

- October 19th, 1924 – A ham station in England was able to work a station in New Zealand, a distance of almost 12,000 miles, one of the first recorded times that ham radio reached halfway around the world (still impressive in my book). It would take a few more years to discover the effect that the Ionosphere plays on Radio.
- October 4th, 1927 - After the creation of the Federal Radio Commission (precursor to the FCC) that same year, an International Radio Telegraph Conference was held in Washington, DC. While amateur radio was truly international, word was filtered out of Europe and the Far East that many governments were definitely ANTI amateur radio. A significant effect of the conference was the 745 nations attending started hammering out international treaties covering the known radio spectrum. The result was the now familiar 160m, 80m, 40m and 20m bands we know and love, and the specific frequencies allowed in each.
- October 1st, 1928 - Another decision by the conference was designation of callsigns (originally called “call signals”). The callsign prefixs for the United States became K, N and W. This made early number starting callsigns (1AW for example) change to K1AW. Of Note, Hiram Percy Maxim was allowed to keep his 1AW callsign until his death in 1936. The history of callsigns are pretty interesting if you have a minute to take a look.
- October 1948 - The October 1948 issue of QST magainze described a new “amplifying crystal”, what we now call a transistor. No one at the time fully realized the impact and potential they would have on electronics and radios.
- October 17th 1972 – The FCC issued an odd ruling in which the new Technician class could only use portions of the 2m band, only the 145-147mhz. So if a repeater was utilized and the output exceeded 147, early Technicians would be operating outside of band privileges. However, that changed on October 17th, when the FCC allowed them to use the 147-148mhz part of the band. They were still denied 10m, 144-145mhz and Novice privileges (which is what we would identify as a technician now).

### Amateur radio event connects Karahantepe to 1,000 global stations

Amateur radio operators broadcasting from Karahantepe—part of the Tas Tepeler Neolithic network in southeastern Turkey—connected with nearly 1,000 stations across 40 countries during a three-day initiative to promote the ancient site globally.

Tas Tepeler, or known as "Stone Mounds" in English, a network of prehistoric settlements centered around Sanliurfa in southeastern Turkey, is developing into an internationally recognized archaeological destination as overseas exhibitions encourage visitors to look beyond Gobeklitepe and explore the wider region.

Members of the Turkish Radio Amateurs Association (TRAC) set up radio equipment and antennas at the Karahantepe Welcome Center in Sanliurfa to bring attention to these historic areas and preservation efforts.

### Amateur Radio To The Rescue!

HONOLULU, HAWAII — September 16, 2026 — Michael Miller, KH6ML, began hiking the Diamond Head Summit Trail on Oahu to complete a combined POTA and SOTA activation Operating portable from the summit with an FX-4CR HF transceiver at approximately 20 watts, a small Bioenno battery, end-fed wire antenna and an AnyTone VHF/UHF handheld, Miller completed 11 contacts. Moments after completing his 11th contact, a visitor approached Miller and asked if he could help. A woman had collapsed inside the observation bunker below the summit.

Miller immediately left his HF station behind, took his handheld radio and headed toward the patient while calling 911. Because of uncertainty about cellular coverage inside the reinforced structure, Miller also advised amateur radio operators with whom he had been communicating to stand by in case an alternate communications path became necessary.

Honolulu Fire Department personnel reached the bunker and assumed care of the patient. Additional firefighters and rescue personnel followed with medical and rescue equipment.

Later that afternoon, Miller received a message from the family thanking him for his assistance and reporting that the woman was doing well and the family had returned to their hotel.

A short video documenting the Diamond Head activation and emergency response is available here: <https://youtu.be/_GO4as2CSvc>

### You Can Now Spend The Night 80 Feet Up In A West Virginia Fire Tower

COWEN, W.Va. — Travelers can now spend the night 80 feet above a remote West Virginia forest, sleeping in a restored fire lookout for as little as $100 a night.

The Red Oak Fire Lookout opened Tuesday for overnight stays in the Monongahela National Forest. Its 14-by-14-foot cab sits atop a steel tower at an elevation of 3,704 feet, and joins a growing number of former fire lookout towers that are being remodeled to AirBnB-type lodging.
Reservations are available through <https://recreation.gov>, where overnight stays start at $100.
